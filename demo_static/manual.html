Section 01

🩸 How Blood Sugar Works

Blood sugar (glucose) is the primary fuel your body uses for energy. When you eat food — especially carbohydrates — your body breaks it down into glucose, which enters your bloodstream. Your pancreas then releases insulin, a hormone that helps cells absorb glucose for energy, bringing blood sugar levels back down.

What the Numbers Mean

Blood glucose is measured in mg/dL (milligrams per decilitre) or mmol/L (millimoles per litre). Here's what different ranges mean:

Range (mg/dL)Range (mmol/L)What It MeansStatus
< 70< 3.9 Too low — Hypoglycaemia. Dangerous. Dizziness, confusion, fainting. ⚠️ Hypo
70 – 1403.9 – 7.8 Normal range. This is the healthy zone your body aims for. ✅ Normal
140 – 1807.8 – 10.0 Elevated. Common 1–2 hours after a meal, especially carb-heavy ones. ⚠️ Elevated
> 180> 10.0 Too high — Hyperglycaemia. If frequent, causes long-term organ damage. 🚨 Hyper

Type 1 Diabetes

In Type 1 Diabetes (T1D), the immune system destroys the cells that make insulin. Without insulin, glucose stays in the blood instead of entering cells, causing blood sugar to stay dangerously high. People with T1D must inject insulin manually (or use a pump) every time they eat, and they must constantly monitor their blood sugar using a Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) — a small sensor worn on the arm that reads glucose every 5 minutes.

💡

Why 60 minutes? We predict 60 minutes into the future because that's enough time for someone to take action — adjust insulin dosage, choose a different meal, or go for a walk — before blood sugar gets too high.

📂 The 3 Datasets

We trained our models on 3 real-world clinical datasets from actual patients. Each dataset was collected differently, from different populations, giving us a diverse training ground.

🏥

HUPA-UCM

🇬🇧

BrisT1D

🥗

CGMacros

45 people who are NOT diabetic. Has full meal details: carbs, protein, fat, fibre, calories. Also has activity data.

What are "Scenarios"?

We tested three progressively richer sets of input data to see if more information improves predictions:

  • Scenario 1 (Carbs Only): Glucose + carbohydrates + insulin. The minimum clinical data.
  • Scenario 2 (Full Macros): Adds protein, fat, fibre, calories. Only available for CGMacros dataset.
  • Scenario 3 (Macros + Activity): Adds heart rate, steps, and activity calories.
📊

Key Finding: Scenario 3 improved results on CGMacros by ~10% (because it has rich meal data), but barely changed results on HUPA/BrisT1D — meaning glucose history alone is already a very powerful predictor.

How We Collected & Harmonised the Data

Each dataset came from a different source in a completely different format. Here's every step we took to make them compatible:

📥 Where Each Dataset Came From

  • HUPA-UCM: Published on Mendeley Data by Hospital Universitario Príncipe de Asturias (Madrid, Spain). Individual CSV files per patient containing timestamped CGM readings, carbohydrate intake, and insulin doses (basal + bolus). Glucose in mg/dL.
  • BrisT1D: Published on Kaggle by the University of Bristol (UK). A single CSV with pre-processed lag columns (e.g., bg-1:00 means glucose 1 hour ago). Also includes heart rate, steps, and calorie data from smart watches. Glucose in mmol/L (×18 to convert to mg/dL).
  • CGMacros: Published on PhysioNet. Non-diabetic participants wore CGM sensors and logged every meal with full nutritional breakdown (carbs, protein, fat, fibre, calories, portion consumed). Also includes Fitbit activity data. Glucose in mg/dL.

🔧 How We Made Them Work Together

Since each dataset has different columns, different time formats, and different available channels, we built a unified dataset builder that converts all three into a standardised format:

  • Time alignment: All data is resampled to a uniform 5-minute grid. If a reading is missing, we forward-fill for gaps ≤ 30 minutes (6 steps), otherwise mark as NaN.
  • Sliding windows: We extract overlapping windows: 72 timesteps of history (6 hours) → predict glucose at t+60 min (12 steps ahead).
  • Channel mapping: Each dataset's raw column names are mapped to unified names (e.g., HUPA's "basal" + "bolus" → "insulin"; BrisT1D's "bg-0:05" → glucose lag 1; CGMacros's per-meal macros → cumulative sums over windows).
  • Feature engineering: For each window, we compute 21–33 features including: current glucose, rolling means (30/60/360 min), standard deviations, slopes (30/60 min linear regression), deltas (5/15/30/60 min change), carb sums over various horizons, and time-of-day encoding (sin/cos of hour).
  • Participant-level splits: Patients are assigned to train/val/test based on an MD5 hash of their ID — ensuring no patient appears in both training and test. This prevents data leakage.
  • Normalisation: Glucose, heart rate, and steps use standard scaling (mean/std). Sparse channels (carbs, protein, fat) use robust scaling (median/IQR) to handle the many zero values.
🔑

Why this matters: By converting 3 completely different data formats into one unified pipeline, we can train the same model architecture on any dataset and directly compare results. This is a key engineering contribution — without this, each dataset would need its own code.

Section 03

🤖 Forecasting Models — GRU & Transformer

We use deep learning — neural networks that learn patterns from data. Specifically, we use two architectures designed for time-series data (data ordered in time, like glucose readings every 5 minutes).

🔄

GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit)

A type of recurrent neural network that processes data one timestep at a time, maintaining a "memory" of what it has seen. Think of it as reading a book: it reads one word at a time and remembers the context of the story so far.

  • How it works: Takes 72 glucose readings one-by-one. At each step, it decides what to "remember" and what to "forget" from its internal state.
  • Strength: Naturally understands sequences. Great at capturing "glucose is currently rising" patterns.
  • Size: ~200,000 parameters (lightweight)
⚡

Transformer Encoder

A neural sequence architecture that looks at all 72 readings simultaneously using self-attention. It asks: "Which past readings are most relevant for predicting the future?"

  • How it works: Computes importance weights between every pair of readings. Can learn, e.g., that glucose 30 min ago matters more than glucose 4 hours ago.
  • Strength: Can capture long-range patterns (like "you ate 3 hours ago").
  • Size: ~500,000 parameters

The Pipeline — How Data Flows Through the Model

📡 Raw CGM Data 72 readings (6 hours)
→
🧹 Preprocessing Clean + normalise + mask
→
🧠 GRU / Transformer Deep neural network
→
🎲 MC-Dropout × 20 20 passes with randomness
→
📊 Prediction + Bands Mean, Q10, Q90
🏆

Our Finding: GRU consistently outperformed Transformer on all 3 datasets. Why? With only 10% training data, the simpler GRU avoids overfitting. Also, glucose forecasting relies heavily on recent readings — exactly what GRU excels at.

Section 04

🎯 Target Mode — Delta vs Absolute

We train models in two different ways to predict the future glucose value. The difference is subtle but important:

📍

Absolute Mode

The model directly predicts: "Blood glucose in 60 minutes will be 165 mg/dL."

  • The model must learn the full range of possible glucose values (40–400+ mg/dL)
  • Can be harder to learn because glucose varies widely between people
📈

Delta Mode (Recommended)

The model predicts the change: "Blood glucose will go UP by 15 mg/dL." Then we add that to the current reading.

  • Easier to learn — changes are typically small (-30 to +30 mg/dL)
  • More stable and accurate in our experiments
  • We add: current (150) + predicted change (+15) = 165 mg/dL
✅

Key Takeaway: Delta mode performs better in almost all cases. On HUPA with Transformer, switching from absolute to delta reduced the error from 35.4 to 27.2 mg/dL — a 23% improvement.

Section 05

🎲 MC-Dropout & Uncertainty Bands

This is the most important innovation in our system. A normal prediction says "glucose will be 165." But how confident is the model? MC-Dropout answers this:

What is Dropout?

Dropout is a technique used when training neural networks. During training, random neurons are temporarily "turned off" (set to zero) on each pass. This prevents the model from memorising specific patterns and forces it to be robust. Normally, dropout is turned OFF during prediction.

What is Monte Carlo Dropout (MC-Dropout)?

Here's the clever trick: we keep dropout ON during prediction. Because different random neurons are disabled each time, each of the 20 forward passes gives a slightly different prediction. The spread of these 20 predictions tells us how certain or uncertain the model is.

1

Feed the same input 20 times

Same 72 glucose readings go into the model 20 times

2

Each time, different neurons are randomly dropped

This produces 20 slightly different predictions, e.g.: 162, 168, 165, 170, 160, 167, ...

3

Calculate statistics from the 20 predictions

Mean = average of all 20 → this is our "best guess" prediction
Q10 = 10th percentile → "optimistic lower bound"
Q90 = 90th percentile → "pessimistic upper bound"
The gap between Q10 and Q90 is the 80% confidence interval

4

Narrow band = confident. Wide band = uncertain.

If all 20 predictions are similar (e.g., 162–168), the model is very sure. If they're spread out (e.g., 140–200), the model is uncertain — proceed with caution!

🧠 Glucose Insights Panel

Below the prediction metrics, the demo shows a trajectory insights panel that analyses the glucose curve and explains the likely clinical context:

What the Insights Panel Shows

  • Current Zone — Is glucose normal, elevated, hypo, or hyper? Includes the exact value and what that zone means clinically.
  • 30-Minute Trend — Is glucose rising, falling, or stable? Shows the rate of change in mg/dL (or mmol/L) per hour. A falling trend at >25 mg/dL/hr suggests active insulin or exercise.
  • 6-Hour Variability — How much has glucose swung over the observation window? Uses Coefficient of Variation (CV%). Low CV (<15%) means stable; high CV (>30%) means big swings.
  • Detected Events — Automatically finds spikes and dips in the 6-hour history. E.g., "Spike detected ~90 min ago — rose by 45 mg/dL."
  • Likely Causes — Based on the trend and events, the system suggests what probably caused the current glucose state:
    • 🍞 Recent Meal — If a spike was detected 30–90 min ago, carbs are the likely cause.
    • 🍕 Delayed Carb Absorption — Spikes 120+ min ago may be from high-fat meals (pizza, fried food).
    • 💉 Insulin Action — If glucose is falling, insulin is likely pulling it down.
    • 🏃 Physical Activity — Exercise increases glucose uptake by muscles.
    • ⚠️ Insufficient Insulin — Rising glucose above 140 may mean the dose didn't cover the meal.
    • ✅ Balanced State — Stable + normal = everything is working as it should.
  • Prediction Breakdown (after running prediction) — Explains in words: how much glucose will change, whether the confidence band is narrow or wide, what the safety score means, and how close the prediction was to the actual value.
Section 07

🍽️ Meal Comparison & Safety Scoring

This is the real-world application — the whole reason we built this system. Imagine you have diabetes and you're choosing between two meals for lunch. Which one will spike your blood sugar less? Our system answers this.

How It Works

🧠 What the Model Actually Sees

It's important to understand: the standard prediction uses only the glucose curve shape. The model doesn't know what the patient ate, their insulin dose, or their activity level. It learned: "curves that look like this usually end up at X in 60 minutes."

The meal comparison adds data — it injects the carbohydrate value into the model's carb input channel at the current timestep, simulating "what if this many grams of carbs were eaten NOW?" This changes the prediction because the model learned the relationship between carb intake and glucose spikes.

Why this matters: The model makes surprisingly accurate predictions from glucose shape alone because glucose history is the strongest predictor (as our results confirm — Scenario 1 often beats S2 and S3). The meal comparison adds the real-world planning dimension.

1

Enter two meal options

Type the name and macros (carbs, protein, fat, fibre) for Meal A and Meal B. E.g., "Grilled Chicken Salad" (15g carbs) vs "Pasta Carbonara" (75g carbs).

2

Model predicts glucose after each meal

The carbohydrate value is injected into the model's carbs channel, simulating "what if you ate this meal right now?" It runs MC-Dropout for each meal independently.

3

Results appear on chart + ranking cards

Each meal appears as a coloured triangle on the chart at t=60: ▲ amber for Meal A, ▲ purple for Meal B. The ranking cards show predicted glucose, confidence band, safety score, and a macro breakdown explaining why (e.g., "High-carb meals cause rapid glucose spikes in 30-90 min").

⚠️

Important: This is a research prototype. The meal simulation is simplified — in a real app, the model would have much richer meal timing and absorption data. The rankings are directionally correct (higher carbs = higher spike) but the absolute glucose values may not be precise.

⏰

Why 6 Hours of History? Why 60-Minute Prediction?

We use 6 hours (72 × 5-min readings) because it captures ~2 meal-insulin cycles while keeping the signal fresh. The 60-minute prediction horizon is clinically actionable — enough time to make a real decision. For a detailed explanation of why this window was chosen (and why alternatives are worse), see Section 10 — Prediction Window Design.

🏆 Key Takeaways

  • GRU wins overall — best or near-best on every dataset. Simpler, trains faster, doesn't overfit with limited data.
  • Delta mode always helps — reduced errors by 10-23% across the board. On HUPA Transformer: abs=35.43 vs delta=27.23 (23% improvement!).

⚠️

Limitation: All deep models trained on 10% of data (hardware constraints — 925 min total runtime, system crashed on full data attempts). With 100% data, we expect 5-15% improvement across all models. This is documented as future work. Baselines (Persistence, Ridge, GBDT) used 100% of training data.

Section 09

⏰ Prediction Window Design — Why 6 Hours → 60 Minutes

One of the most important design decisions in glucose forecasting is how much historical data to feed the model and how far into the future to predict. This section explains our rationale.

The Input Window: 6 Hours (72 × 5-min readings)

📐 Why Exactly 6 Hours?

6 hours is a well-established window in glucose forecasting literature — it captures approximately 2 full meal-insulin cycles while keeping the signal fresh and relevant.

  • Physiological basis: Rapid-acting insulin peaks at 60–90 minutes and wears off by ~4 hours. A 6-hour window captures the full lifecycle of at least one, often two, insulin doses.
  • Meal coverage: Most people eat meals roughly 3–4 hours apart, so 6 hours covers ~2 complete meal digestion cycles including the postprandial glucose response (the spike after eating).
  • 72 readings at 5-min intervals provides high temporal resolution — the model can detect rapid changes (e.g., glucose rising at 3 mg/dL per minute after a sugary drink).

Why Not Less Data? (1–2 Hours)

🔍 Too Little Context

  • Misses delayed fat absorption: High-fat meals (pizza, fried food) cause glucose to rise 2–4 hours after eating. A 1-hour window would completely miss this pattern.
  • Misses overnight drift: During sleep, the liver slowly releases glucose (the "dawn phenomenon" in T1D). A short window fails to capture this gradual trend.
  • Insufficient trend context: Is glucose rising because of a meal, exercise recovery, or insulin wearing off? The model needs ~3–4 hours of context to distinguish these causes.
  • Only 12–24 data points (at 5-min intervals) — not enough for the GRU to learn meaningful temporal patterns.

Why Not More Data? (12–24 Hours)

📉 Diminishing Returns

  • Glucose dynamics are dominated by the last 1–2 hours: Recent meals, currently active insulin, and recent exercise have by far the strongest influence on where glucose will be in 60 minutes.
  • Signal-to-noise ratio drops: What you ate for breakfast 18 hours ago has essentially zero predictive power for your next-hour glucose. Including it adds noise without signal.
  • Computational cost: Longer sequences mean more memory and slower training. A 24-hour window (288 steps) would quadruple the sequence length without proportional accuracy gains.
  • The model confirms this: GRU gates naturally learn to weigh recent readings more heavily than distant ones. We tested this implicitly — the learned gate activations show the model pays most attention to the last ~2 hours.
🧪

Evidence from our experiments: The GRU gate mechanism inherently learns temporal importance weights. Recent readings (0–2 hours ago) dominated the hidden-state representation, while readings from 4–6 hours ago contributed weakly. This validates 6 hours as an effective upper bound — enough context to be useful, without drowning the model in irrelevant data.

Why Not "Full X Days → Predict Future Days"?

🚫 The Day-Ahead Forecasting Myth

Some research approaches use entire days (or weeks) of data to predict glucose trends for the next day. While academically interesting, this approach is unrealistic for clinical use:

  • People need real-time decisions: A patient standing in front of a restaurant menu needs to know "should I eat this NOW?" — not "what will my average glucose look like tomorrow."
  • Future meals are unknown: Predicting glucose 24 hours ahead requires knowing what you'll eat — which you don't know until you decide. Our approach treats the next meal as a variable to explore (the Meal Comparison feature).
  • Insulin timing is unpredictable: For T1D patients, future insulin doses depend on future meals, which haven't happened yet. Only the currently-active insulin dose is known.
  • Compounding errors: The further into the future you predict, the more errors compound. At 60 minutes, our model achieves ~23 mg/dL error. At 24 hours, errors would likely exceed 80+ mg/dL — clinically useless.
  • Actionability window: Clinically, 60 minutes is the sweet spot — long enough to take preventive action (eat, inject, exercise), but short enough that the prediction is still reliable.
✅

Summary: Our design — 6 hours history → 60 minutes forecast — is grounded in both physiological reasoning and empirical validation. It captures the recent metabolic context that matters most, discards stale data that adds noise, and produces predictions within a clinically actionable timeframe. The GRU naturally learned to confirm this by weighting recent data most heavily.
