Kaggle Competition Dataset

BrisT1D — Blood Glucose Prediction Dataset

What is this dataset? The BrisT1D dataset was created by researchers at the University of Bristol and published as a Kaggle competition. It contains real-world data from people living with Type 1 Diabetes — a condition where the body cannot produce insulin, so patients must manually manage their blood sugar through insulin injections and careful meal planning.

How is the data structured? Unlike raw time-series data, BrisT1D has already been pre-engineered into a tabular format designed for machine learning. Each row represents a single prediction opportunity. For each row, the dataset provides 6 hours of historical data (the "lookback window") sampled every 5 minutes, giving 72 time steps across 7 signal channels:

• Blood Glucose (BG) — from a Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) worn on the body, measured in mmol/L
• Insulin — from an insulin pump, recording how much insulin was delivered
• Carbohydrates (Carbs) — manually logged meals, in grams
• Heart Rate (HR) — from a smartwatch
• Steps — daily step count from the smartwatch
• Calories — estimated energy expenditure
• Activity Labels — categorical labels like "Walking", "Running", "Yoga", etc.

The prediction target is bg+1:00 — the patient's blood glucose reading 1 hour into the future. This is clinically meaningful because it gives patients enough lead time to adjust their food or insulin.

Column naming convention: Columns follow the pattern feature-H:MM, e.g. bg-2:30 means "blood glucose reading from 2 hours and 30 minutes ago". This creates 72 columns per feature × 7 features = 504 feature columns, plus metadata columns (patient ID, time of day) and the target. Total: 508 columns.

Train/test split: The training set has 9 participants and 177,024 rows. The test set has 15 participants, of which 7 are completely unseen during training — meaning the model must generalize across different people, a key real-world challenge.

What this means for modeling: The ML model must be robust to missing values. Imputation strategies (filling in gaps) or architectures that handle sparse data natively (like transformers with masking) will be important.

Figure 08

Correlation Heatmap (Engineered Features)

This heatmap shows the correlation between engineered summary features. A correlation of +1 means two features move perfectly together, -1 means they move in opposite directions, and 0 means no linear relationship.

Features were engineered by summing or averaging signals within time windows:
• carbs_30/60/120 — total carbs consumed in the last 30, 60, or 120 minutes
• insulin_30/60/120 — total insulin delivered in those windows
• hr_mean_60 — average heart rate in the last hour
• steps_60 — total steps in the last hour
• delta_bg — the change in blood glucose (target minus current)

Key finding: current_bg is the strongest predictor of bg+1:00 (correlation ≈ 0.94) — your current glucose is the best predictor of your near-future glucose. However, delta_bg (the change) has much weaker correlations, meaning predicting the direction and magnitude of change is the harder, more interesting problem.

Figure 12

Activity vs. Blood Glucose Change (ΔBG)

This chart shows the average blood glucose change (ΔBG) associated with each activity. Red bars indicate activities where blood glucose tends to drop (negative ΔBG), and green bars where it tends to rise.

ΔBG = target_BG − current_BG. A ΔBG of −4 means blood glucose dropped by 4 mmol/L on average when that activity was occurring.

Gentle activities like Walking and Yoga are associated with slight glucose increases — possibly because the participants walk or do yoga after eating, and the meal effect outweighs the exercise effect.
Mendeley Data — Preprocessed

HUPA-UCM Diabetes Dataset

What is this dataset? The HUPA-UCM dataset was collected at the Hospital Universitario Príncipe de Asturias (HUPA) in collaboration with the Universidad Complutense de Madrid (UCM) in Spain, and published on Mendeley Data. It contains continuous physiological recordings from 25 real patients with Type 1 Diabetes over a median of 13.3 days per patient.

How was the data collected? Each patient wore three devices simultaneously:
1. A Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) — a small sensor inserted under the skin that measures blood glucose every few minutes, in mg/dL (milligrams per decilitre).
2. An insulin pump — a device that delivers insulin continuously (basal rate) and in bursts (bolus) at meal times.
3. A smartwatch — tracking heart rate, steps, and estimated calorie burn.

The preprocessed version (which we use here) has already been cleaned and resampled to 5-minute intervals. Each patient's data is in a separate CSV file (e.g., HUPA0001P.csv), and all files share the same 8 columns:

• time — ISO timestamp (e.g., 2018-06-13T18:40:00)
• glucose — CGM reading in mg/dL (to convert to mmol/L, divide by 18)
• calories — estimated calorie expenditure per 5-minute interval
• heart_rate — average heart rate (bpm) in the interval
• steps — step count in the interval
• basal_rate — continuous background insulin delivery (units/hour)
• bolus_volume_delivered — discrete insulin bolus (units), non-zero at meal times
• carb_input — carbohydrates consumed (grams), non-zero at meals

Why this dataset is different from BrisT1D: While BrisT1D gives pre-engineered flat rows (one row = one prediction), the HUPA data is raw time series. We must construct the prediction windows ourselves — which is exactly what the supervised dataset builder in our analysis does. This demonstrates that we can transform any diabetes time-series into the same input format, making our thesis approach generalizable.

A pleasant surprise: The preprocessed data has zero missingness — every cell in every column is filled. This is unusual for real-world health data and means we can build supervised ML samples with no data loss, yielding 307,317 usable samples.

Figure 08

Glucose Response to Meals (Event-Aligned Curves)

This is one of the most important thesis plots. It shows what happens to blood glucose before and after a meal.

How it works: We identified 2,541 meal events (rows where carb_input > 0), then for each event we extracted the glucose trace from 120 minutes before to 240 minutes after. All traces are aligned so that minute 0 = the meal event. The blue line shows the average, and the light blue band shows the 25th–75th percentile range.

The classic meal response: Before the meal (negative minutes), glucose is relatively stable or slightly declining. Right around the meal, there's a brief dip — likely because patients often take an insulin bolus just before or as they eat. Then glucose rises over the next 30–90 minutes as the food is digested and absorbed. Eventually it levels off as the insulin takes effect.

Thesis significance: This clear, reproducible meal → glucose-rise pattern is exactly what our ML model needs to learn. Given the meal signal (carb_input) and the pre-meal glucose trajectory, the model should predict the post-meal glucose trend.
Figure 09

Glucose Response to Insulin Bolus

The counterpart to the meal response, this plot shows what happens to glucose when an insulin bolus is administered. We found 3,415 bolus events (rows where bolus_volume_delivered > 0) and extracted the same ±2h/+4h glucose trace around each event.

Bolus insulin is a fast-acting insulin injection that patients give themselves (usually via their pump) before or during a meal to counteract the glucose rise from food. It typically takes effect within 15–30 minutes and peaks at 1–2 hours.

The green curve shows that glucose tends to drop or flatten after a bolus, demonstrating the corrective effect of insulin.

Important nuance: Bolus events often coincide with meals, so the glucose may initially rise from food and then fall from insulin. The model needs to learn the interaction between carbs and insulin, not treat them independently.
Figure 10

Per-Participant Meal Responses

This grid shows the meal glucose response for 6 individual participants. Each faint colored line is one meal event, and the black line is the average for that participant.

This reveals how individual variation affects meal responses. Some patients have dramatic post-meal spikes (large glucose excursions), while others stay remarkably flat. The number of meal events (shown in each title) also varies widely between participants.

Key observation: The variability in meal responses between individuals (different average curves) is as large as the variability within a single individual (spread of colored lines). A strong model should capture both sources of variation.

PhysioNet Dataset

CGMacros — High-Resolution Diet & Glucose Monitoring

What is this dataset? CGMacros combines Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM) with detailed photographic macronutrient tracking and wearable activity data. Unlike the T1D datasets, this dataset involves healthy or Type 2 participants without insulin pumps.

Why is it important for the thesis? This dataset is essential for "Scenario 2: full macros" and "Scenario 3: macros + activity". Unlike HUPA/BrisT1D which only consistently logs carbs, CGMacros breaks down meals into Carbs, Protein, Fat, Fiber, and Amount Consumed.

Target Variable: libre_gl (Libre sensor), selected due to the lowest missingness across participants.

ML Engineering

Thesis Pipeline Progress (Steps 0–2)

Step 0: Evaluation Protocol ✅

Established a rigorous, leakage-safe protocol:

  • Split Policy: Deterministic participant-level hashing for HUPA and CGMacros; GroupKFold for BrisT1D.
  • Delta Target Training: Models are trained to predict both absolute glucose and the change in glucose (delta). The better performer is selected.
  • Metrics: Primary ranking by MAE, supported by RMSE and clinical range-stratified MAE (Hypo, Normal, Hyper).

Step 1: Unified Dataset Builder ✅

Transformed all three raw datasets into standardized (X, y, mask) tensors with shape (72, C). Applied causal imputation (forward-fill for gaps ≤30m) and computed time-of-day cyclic features.

Step 2: Strong Baselines ✅

Implemented and evaluated four baseline models across all datasets and scenarios using 30 engineered temporal features (rolling means, slopes, interval sums):

  • Persistence: y_hat = glucose(t). The absolute minimum bar.
  • Linear Drift: Extrapolates using the rolling slope over the last 60 minutes.
  • Ridge Regression: Tests the linear value of engineered window features.
  • GBDT (HistGradientBoostingRegressor): The strongest tabular baseline, capable of capturing nonlinear interactions without explicit crossing.
